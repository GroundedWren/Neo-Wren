Slingshot

Prompt: Who fucking threw that?

By Vera, November 19, 2026

I’m pulled from sleep by the sharp ring of antler on flint — mom hardly ever wakes me up with a hand on my shoulder these days, it’s more often just the sound of her beginning to work that stirs me. I roll over and cocoon myself in the soft, warm furs — they feel wonderful while the chilly air nips at my cheeks. I groan, half to signal my wakefulness and half just because it feels nice to do.

But oh, maybe mom will think I’m complaining about the noise — that’s not at all what I mean! I roll over to face her and smile as I stretch; she looks up at me from her hunched seat in the knapping corner, gentleness and tiredness in her eyes. I hear her voice, good morning, sweetie.

Morning, I reply as I rub the sleepy from my eyes. She smiles at me, then resumes gently tapping at the flint knife with the antler, sluffing off thin layers of dulled rock to bring it back to sharpness. She’s hauled in a hard lump of stump, jagged and unshaped for now but soon to be a spear to replace the one that’d snapped yesterday under a fallen boar (which, cooked slowly over the hearth with a couple handfuls of herbs I’d gathered myself, was absolutely delicious). Maybe that’ll be breakfast.

I throw off the furs and stand — wow, the air is really biting! When did it get so cold? I wrap my arms around myself and vocalize a little question at mom.

Outside, go look, she says, gesturing with the half-sharpened lithic. Jolted awake, I dart over to the rock shelter’s entrance and peek out through the hide curtains.

My heart skips three full beats. Snow?? I squeal with excitement and dash out into the bright wonderland, the soft powder pushing up between my bare toes as big, fat flakes float through the air around me.

Mom calls after — Skaut come back in! You’ll catch cold! I should listen, but I'm too caught up in the moment. I hold my hands out and twirl around, the snow whizzing across my vision in brilliant white lines, whiter than any bone, white as river churn.

Skaut, don’t make me ask you again! she says with just a hint of warning in her growling tone. Right, right, I should listen.

I burst back in through the curtains and feel my smile pulling tightly at my cheeks. Mom! Mom! It’s so pretty! Can you believe it?? I yell, jumping up and down.

Dad groans from his bed toward the back of our little cavity in the hill — I don’t think it’s in response to me, but it’s a little annoying that he’s dampening the mood right now; can’t we just enjoy the moment? I haven’t seen snow since I was a little stumbler, long enough ago that I hardly remember what it was like. This is so exciting!

Mom sighs. Yes darling, it’s very pretty. Please don’t yell — you know your dad is in pain. I see a sadness in her eyes; I know she doesn’t like to dampen my excitement. I have so many memories of her getting just as aflutter as me when we find the perfect river stone, or a bush of the really good berries, or when she and the other hunters come back with a big pile of juicy meat all ready to tear apart.

Still, she’s right. I feel a little twinge of guilt — dad is in such pain that it’s hard to think about, I don’t want to. He hasn’t been able to leave home — or even walk — ever since that stag-moose he killed fell onto him and hurt his leg. I’ve never seen anything like it, how purple and swollen his thigh has gotten, and how long it’s taking for him to feel better. Like, when I was smaller I snapped my pinky crossing the river, but after just a few days of pain (I was very tough!) it went back to normal, though it might be just a little crooked. I don’t think I’ve ever seen mom so worried either — sometimes, after the fire’s burned out and she thinks I’m sleeping, I’ve feel her sob silently and pull me in close. She’s been working so hard too — everyone has — to make up for all that he can’t do right now. He’s the best hunter in the group, mom’s always told me with pride, but it’s just made his absence felt all the more.

I also, miss him, I think. He hasn’t been quite the same since the injury — we used to laugh and play constantly, but now he doesn’t even smile. His head gets so warm and confused and he says such mean things to me and mom; she’s told me he doesn’t mean it, and I believe her, but it still hurts. I want him back so badly.

I snap out of my thoughts and smile. Sorry, I grunt, and give her a little hug. Can I go back out if I put my warm furs on, please?

She smiles up at me. Sure, sweetie. Just be careful — snow is slippery. I don’t want you falling. I nod dutifully. And come back by high sun — I’ll need your help pulling the tendons out of the pig. He’s a particularly sinewy one; we’ll be able to make you a proper hammer out of them later.

Yes mom, I hum as I work on pulling my furs over my head. Before I head out, I remember my project — I bet it’d work great with the snow! I reach under my pillow and pull it out, taking a second to admire my handiwork. It’s fastened from the cleanest, biggest wishbone I’ve ever found with a long wooden handle secured tightly with boar tendons just like how mom showed me. I’ve carved little designs into the bony “Y” with my knife, and I run my thumb along them now — memories of little trees, rabbits, and elk reflect their presence in the tactile grooves. It’s probably my best work, even mom says so! I pull at the deerskin I’ve fastened to the top of the bone’s splitting; it forms a little stretchy pouch that can send small stones absolutely hurling at things. I know mom doesn’t really get it (yet), but I really like my invention! Maybe someday she’ll let me try hunting with it.

I pop the little contraption into my pouch and head out into the world — bye mom, bye dad! I’ll be back!

Love, dad manages with a weak little grunt.

I’ve had a wonderful time running through the trees and scooping the snow up into little shapes, but now I’m missing someone to share it with. There aren’t any kids my age in our group, except Varn, and I don’t want to play with him. I think a moment. I bet Nara (at least, that’s what I think her name is) would enjoy this! Mom’s warned me about the tall, thin people on the other side of the lake — I don’t think she trusts them — but Nara’s been nothing but nice to me! Unlike mom, I don’t really care that they have small eyes or weird foreheads or thin fingers; they’re still people! I really don’t need to understand all the things she says either, I know that she’s my friend.

Yeah, I’ll go find her. I almost get lost as I navigate the path around the lake; everything looks so different with the snow on the ground. My nose is running and my toes are numb by the time I see the blue smoke of their camp, but I’m not nearly ready to go back inside yet, not until I’ve found her.

As expected, she’s also out here playing around — tracking is so easy with this snow stuff. And she’s alone. Oh, oh I have a terrible idea.

I squat down and form a couple big handfuls of snow into a tight ball; I bet this’ll fly just as well as the pebbles. I’ve gotten to be a pretty good shot — if I can hit a tree from the far side of the river with a pebble, surely I can hit Nara from across the clearing. Hehehe, this is gonna be so funny!

I load the ball into the seat of my invention and pull it way back, locking my left elbow with the bone in my outstretched hand pointing riiight at the back of her head.

Splat! She nearly falls forward, then spins around, eyes darting around wildly as her shoulders hunch up with the chill. Who fucking threw that? she yells, a fierce hunger for revenge in her little brown eyes.

I giggle and burst out from the trees, screaming a fierce battle cry as I charge my friend. She takes a startled step back, but I can see the smile on her face as I tackle her at the waist and bring us both down onto the cold snow.
